How to verify a claim yourself
Practical step-by-step walkthroughs for common verification tasks
🖼️Verifying a viral image
  1. Right-click the image → Search image (Chrome/Edge) or upload to Google Lens or TinEye.
  2. Sort results by oldest date — you are looking for the earliest known appearance of this image.
  3. Check whether the context matches: is it being used to describe a different event, country, or time period than the original?
  4. Install the InVID/WeVerify browser plugin — breaks videos into keyframes for reverse image searching of each frame.
  5. Run the image through FotoForensics to detect pixel-level manipulation or cloning.
  6. Check image metadata with ExifTool — may reveal original camera, GPS location, and date.
  7. Use SunCalc to verify claimed location and time by matching shadow angles to sun position.
📌 Verify images before believing captions. The image may be real — the story around it may not be.
💬Checking a political quote
  1. Search the exact phrase in quotation marks: "the exact words" — if it does not appear in news archives, be suspicious.
  2. Find the primary source: official transcript, C-SPAN clip, Hansard record, or verified press release.
  3. Check PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and Snopes — major quotes are often already verified.
  4. Read the full surrounding context — quotes are frequently truncated mid-sentence to reverse their meaning.
  5. Examine any screenshot for font inconsistencies, unusual spacing, or mismatched UI elements that suggest editing.
  6. Use Wayback Machine to retrieve deleted or silently edited official statements.
  7. Check Fact-Check Insights — global structured database of verified claims.
💰Tracing funding & ownership
  1. Look up U.S. political money at OpenSecrets; media ownership at Ground News.
  2. Search organization name + "990" — U.S. nonprofits must file public IRS 990s disclosing donors. Find them at ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer.
  3. Check ICANN WHOIS for domain registration — anonymized registrations on recently created domains are a red flag.
  4. Look up the domain on Wayback Machine — has the site changed name or mission recently?
  5. Check InfluenceWatch for organizational funding profiles and undisclosed affiliations.
  6. Ask: who benefits financially or politically if this claim is believed?
📄Locating original documents
  1. Never rely on a summary — find the primary source: court filings, government reports, peer-reviewed papers, official statements.
  2. U.S. government documents: GovInfo.gov, Congress.gov, PACER (court records).
  3. Academic research: Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar — verify peer review status and funding source.
  4. Leaked and FOIA documents: MuckRock, DocumentCloud, WikiLeaks search.
  5. If a document is cited but not linked — that is a warning sign. Always demand the source link.
  6. Use Wayback Machine to retrieve documents that have been deleted or quietly altered.
🎭Detecting manipulated context
  1. Ask first: is the content real but the context false? Real images and real quotes are frequently paired with invented descriptions.
  2. Check publication date vs. the event described — old content resurfaces constantly during new crises to manufacture outrage.
  3. Be alert to emotional amplification: content engineered to produce anger, fear, or disgust often bypasses critical thinking by design.
  4. Cross-reference across ideologically different outlets — if only one political side is reporting something, investigate why.
  5. Use Hoaxy to visualize how a claim is spreading and who is amplifying it.
  6. Check EUvsDisinfo and Lead Stories for known narrative manipulation patterns.
  7. Check AFP Fact Check methodology to understand how professional verification handles context manipulation.
🗃️Archiving evidence
  1. Archive before sharing or citing — content gets deleted fast. Use Wayback Machine Save, archive.today, or Perma.cc (AFP-recommended for evidence trails).
  2. Take timestamped screenshots — include the URL bar, date, and surrounding context visible in the frame.
  3. Save the full URL with timestamp — URLs often contain metadata about publication date and original source.
  4. For social media: screenshot the post and the account profile page including follower counts and account creation date.
  5. Compare page versions with Diffchecker or monitor ongoing changes with Versionista.
  6. Document your verification chain: keep notes on every source checked, including dead ends — this is how professional investigators work.
📌 Archive before sharing. If you did not save it, it may disappear before anyone else can verify it.
